# Deletion test

Ask of each clause: if this clause were deleted, would the reader still understand the result or
still be able to take the required action? If yes, delete it. If no, keep it.

The test is per clause, not per sentence. A sentence can lose half its words and keep its meaning.

## Worked examples

### 1. A reply that restates the request

Before: `Thanks for your message about the pricing change. I've reviewed the details you shared and I'm happy to confirm that the new per-seat price takes effect from 1 December.`

After: `The per-seat price changes on 1 December.`

What was deleted: the thanks, the restatement, and `happy to confirm`. The result is unchanged; only
the courtesy and the process are gone. Three clauses failed the test; one passed.

### 2. A progress sentence

Before: `I've checked the two supplier pages, opened the price tables, compared the terms, and I'm now able to tell you that the annual price is USD 4,200 for both.`

After: `Both suppliers charge USD 4,200 a year.`

What was deleted: the method. The method belongs in the evidence line, not in the result sentence.
Test: deleting the method does not stop the reader understanding the price.

### 3. A hedge that hides a gap

Before: `It is likely that the delivery window is around five days, although this may vary.`

After: `Delivery window: not found. The supplier page shows no window; the one action that would settle it is the supplier's order confirmation.`

What was deleted: the guess and the two qualifiers. Test: the reader now knows there is no number,
which is more useful than a number that cannot be checked.

### 4. A claim that needs its source

Before: `Our support is the fastest in the market.`

After: `Support answers in a median of 41 minutes (first response, October 2026; sample 1,200 tickets).`

What was deleted: the comparative claim without a measurement. Test: the reader now has a fact they
can check, and the sentence no longer needs a baseline it cannot name.

## When the test is unclear

- If the clause reassures rather than informs, delete it.
- If the clause records a decision the reader must know, keep it, and say `decided`.
- If the clause is a required disclosure, keep it even when it fails the test.
- If deleting the clause would change the meaning of a quotation, keep the quotation intact and cut
  somewhere else.
